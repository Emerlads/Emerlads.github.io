    Georeferencing drone captures raw imagery to 3D Tiles at true coordinates

    Pix4D and similar tools will georeference a capture for you, but the process is closed and the accuracy is whatever they say it is. I rebuilt the whole path with open tooling so each stage can be inspected and the error can be measured.

    Structure-from-Motion hands you camera poses in an arbitrary frame at an arbitrary scale. Nothing downstream knows where on Earth it is or how big it is. So before any training happens, I fit the solved camera centres onto their EXIF GPS positions with a similarity transform and land the whole model in a local ENU frame measured in metres. Train on that, and the output is metric by construction rather than scaled into place afterwards.

    Two numbers, from two different captures. On one site, 119 of the 120 cameras solved and fitted their GPS priors with a 0.107 m horizontal residual. On a smaller 18-camera capture, the 3D camera-fit RMSE came in at about 0.24 m.

    About those numbers

    Both are camera-prior fit residuals. They say how closely the solved cameras agree with the GPS priors they were fitted to, which is not the same thing as accuracy against surveyed checkpoints held back from the fit. My tooling prints that caveat on every report, because the distinction gets lost constantly and only one of the two is an accuracy claim.

    The bug I lost the most time to was vertical. Models were sitting tens of metres above the terrain while the horizontal fit looked flawless. GPS altitude is height above the WGS84 ellipsoid, and I was treating it as height above sea level. It fails quietly, which is what made it hard.

    The geodesy is written out rather than handed to a library, partly so I understood it:

    # geodetic to ECEF on the WGS84 ellipsoid
a  = 6378137.0
f  = 1.0 / 298.257223563
e2 = f * (2.0 - f)
n  = a / math.sqrt(1.0 - e2 * sin_lat * sin_lat)

x = (n + h) * cos_lat * math.cos(lon)
y = (n + h) * cos_lat * math.sin(lon)
z = (n * (1.0 - e2) + h) * sin_lat

# then ECEF to local ENU via the rotation at the scene origin
R = np.array([
    [-sin_lon,            cos_lon,           0.0],
    [-sin_lat * cos_lon, -sin_lat * sin_lon, cos_lat],
    [ cos_lat * cos_lon,  cos_lat * sin_lon, sin_lat],
])

    Eight command-line tools came out of this, about 1,340 lines, published at github.com/Emerlads/georef-splat-tools under MIT.

    Teaching a classifier what matters human-in-the-loop labelling, 1.3M Gaussians

    Unsupervised clustering will label a whole scene, but it finds the classes the data suggests rather than the ones an inspector cares about. So I built a supervised path where an operator corrects labels during a QA pass and the model learns the categories that actually mean something on site.

    Best evidence it worked: during QA I created a class for dead vegetation that no clustering run had ever produced. It matters, because dead vegetation is a fire risk signal, and it exists only because someone who knew that was looking at the scene. Coverage dropped to 72% against 99% unsupervised, which is the honest trade. Supervision only labels what it has been shown, and a second QA round closes most of the gap.

    Classified features export to 3D Tiles and GeoJSON so they can be checked in ArcGIS rather than trapped in my viewer.

    Inspection tools on top of splats pass/fail answers from a photoreal scene

    A photoreal capture is nice to look at and answers nothing on its own. What inspection crews need is measurement, so I built a browser viewer that returns specific determinations and attaches them to the geometry.

    For vegetation clearance it segments whatever you click, computes the lowest point of that object above the ground plane, compares against a threshold, and shows pass or fail with the measured height and a visible clearance plane. A separate mode slices the scene at 1.3 m for diameter at breast height, clusters the trunk cross-sections it finds, and sorts them into NFFL fire fuel size classes by diameter.

    My first approach was to recolour individual splats through the renderer's RGBA buffer. It rendered nothing. The WASM turned out to be embedded as base64 inside the module rather than shipped alongside it, and a plugin I had added to fix that was quietly breaking the build. I gave up on tinting and generated real mesh geometry at each detected feature instead, which rendered correctly and also made the results clickable. Better outcome than the one I was aiming for.
