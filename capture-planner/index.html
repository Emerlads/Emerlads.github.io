Why these defaults

Photogrammetry vs splats

A nadir grid at 75% front and 65% side overlap gives the bundle adjustment what it needs for an orthomosaic and a DSM: every ground point in roughly nine frames, strong along-track geometry, little wasted flight time. That is the mapping default and it is still right for maps.

Gaussian splatting is a different problem. It is optimised to reproduce appearance from the viewpoints it was trained on. A model built from nadir frames looks fine from above and falls apart the moment you orbit it: vertical faces, undersides of canopy and anything that was only ever seen edge-on turn into smeared, floating Gaussians. The fix is to fly the viewpoints the viewer will use, which means obliques, at several heights, all the way around.

So the splat preset raises overlap to 80/70 or higher and adds the ring. More overlap because Structure-from-Motion has to register oblique frames whose scale changes across the image, and because splat training is unforgiving of pose error. Rings at several tiers because one tier leaves a band of the object seen from only one elevation angle. Rings at a shallow pitch (about 30°) see the sides; steep tiers (60–70°) tie the sides to the top; a nadir pass over the same site is still worth flying for scale and for the ground.

Height tiers and pitch

The tiers come from the field standard my team flew: a low obstacle-clearance tier set by the tallest thing on site plus margin, a mid tier, and a high tier under the 400 ft ceiling. Pitch gets steeper as you climb so every tier is still looking at the object rather than past it. The POI height matters: aim at the middle of a tree, not its base, or the low ring photographs trunk and grass.

Keep the orbit speed low (2–4 m/s) and the stop-and-go turn mode on. Obliques at speed smear more than nadir frames because the far edge of the frame is moving faster across the sensor than the near edge.

GSD is the pixel's footprint on the ground, not the smallest thing you can detect. Reliably identifying a feature needs it to span several pixels, so a 2 cm branch needs about 0.5–0.7 cm GSD. For a Mavic 3E, 2.7 cm/px at 100 m; halve the altitude and you halve the GSD but quadruple the photo count.

The warning fires at 400 ft AGL, which caps the Mavic 3E at about 3.2 cm/px. Terrain changes AGL under you; the exported heights are relative to take-off, so on sloped sites use terrain-follow in Pilot 2 or plan from the highest ground.

Lighting, RTK, shutter

Lighting. Flat, bright overcast is the best light for splats and for anything you intend to classify: no hard shadows to bake into the model, no exposure swings between lines. In clear weather fly within about two hours either side of solar noon so shadows are short and stable, and never straddle the moment sun comes out or goes in. Multispectral adds a calibration panel capture before and after, and a stricter window near noon so irradiance is steady.

RTK. Standard GNSS tags each frame to 2–5 m; RTK brings it to 1–3 cm. For the Sim3 fit that places a reconstruction on Earth that is the difference between a fit you can trust and one you have to check with ground control. Even without a base station, enable RTK with a network service if coverage allows. The WPML template marks positioning as GPS unless you toggle RTK in the export.

Shutter. The 0.7 s minimum interval is the mechanical shutter on the M3E class. The planner caps speed so photo spacing never asks for less. Electronic-shutter cameras (M4T wide, consumer Mavics) take photos faster but skew moving scenes; fly them slower rather than faster.
