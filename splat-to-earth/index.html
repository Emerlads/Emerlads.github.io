The problem. Drone teams can now make photoreal 3D models in an afternoon, but those models usually live in a trainer's private coordinate frame. You can't overlay them on a GIS map, measure them against parcels, or hand them to anyone who needs to know where something is. Getting them onto the Earth is fiddly, and getting it wrong is silent.

What this page shows. Two drone splats placed at true latitude, longitude and height in CesiumJS, each checked against independent USGS data. Nothing was nudged into place by hand.

  • LA County Arboretum: 2,046 RTK photos from a DJI Mavic 3M, trained in Postshot and exported in its own swapped-axis frame. I recovered that frame by matching it to the dense cloud and chained it to GPS.
  • Aukerman Park, Ohio: a deliberately weak public dataset (77 nadir photos from 2016, CC0) used to stress-test every step.

The 34-metre trap

GPS height is measured from the WGS84 ellipsoid, a smooth mathematical surface. Maps and terrain models usually use height above mean sea level, which follows the geoid. In this part of Ohio the geoid sits 33.9 m below the ellipsoid.

EXIF doesn't say which one a drone wrote, and vendors differ. Pick wrong and the model sinks or floats by the full geoid height. The horizontal fit still looks perfect, so nothing warns you. Use the third button above to see it.

This camera wrote ellipsoidal heights. I didn't assume that; I tested it. Against USGS 3DEP lidar the GPS-only model sits 1.3 m low. With the geoid applied it would sit 35 m low.

Where the splat is lying

A splat is trained to look right from the camera positions, not to put its surface in the right place. On low-texture ground like this mown field, it can reproduce the grass colour with Gaussians floating well above the real ground. From above it looks like a perfect orthophoto. Tilt the view and the field turns into a dark haze: you're looking through those floaters.

So I audit it. The splat is rendered straight down by alpha compositing, the way a viewer would draw it, and compared on a 0.5 m grid with COLMAP multi-view stereo from the same photos. Both surfaces are also checked against USGS 3DEP lidar at the same ground checkpoints, picked from the sparse SfM points independently of either surface. The standard splat floats over most of the site, and it's no better on open ground (68% floating) than over tree canopy (75%). That's after the delivery filter already removed floaters more than 32 m up. Starting training from the stereo cloud instead of the sparse tie points, with the same photos and the same settings, cut the floating surface from 70% to 54% and halved the height error. That's better, but still not a measurement surface. Uncoloured cells in the audit layer are where the stereo cloud has no data to compare against. Anyone measuring heights, clearances or volumes off a splat inherits that error silently. The Arboretum shows what a good capture buys: with 2,046 photos including obliques, the photoreal splat's open-ground surface sits at a median +0.03 m from the dense cloud, and only 32% of it floats. Still, its per-cell spread is metres, and over canopy it reads about 4 m low, where light passes through the leaves. That's why the defensible-space screener measures from the stereo cloud and uses the splat only for visuals. The capture fix is oblique orbits; this flight was nadir-only.
